# Activity 8a: Ownership Debate

**Week 8 — Tuesday, in class (~30 minutes)**
**Format**: structured debate, 4 groups, then full-class debrief

---

## Overview

A new song has been generated by an AI music model. The song is good — good enough that it is being streamed, played on radio, and generating licensing interest. Who owns it?

Your group has been assigned one of four positions. Your job is to argue that position as persuasively as you can, regardless of whether you personally agree with it. After each group states their position, there is an open debate period. The session closes with a straw poll — at that point you may vote your personal view, not your assigned position.

---

## The Scenario

A music AI company trained a large model on 10 million songs, most of which were used without explicit permission from their creators. A user signed up for the service, typed a prompt: *"a soulful R&B ballad in the style of 1990s Motown with a live-sounding horn section and a female vocal lead"*, clicked generate, listened to three results, and chose the best one. That output is now being licensed.

---

## The Four Positions

### Group 1 — The Human Artist

You are a session musician and songwriter whose recordings were scraped and used in the training data without your knowledge or consent. You did not opt in. Your voice, your style, your lifetime of work contributed to this model's ability to generate music that sounds like you.

**Your argument**: I own a share of this output, or I am owed compensation, or this use of my work was illegal and the output should not be monetizable.

**Key points to develop**:
- What exactly did the model learn from your work?
- Is "style" ownable? What about sound recordings?
- What legal frameworks (copyright, right of publicity, sui generis database rights) might support your claim?

### Group 2 — The AI Company

You invested hundreds of millions of dollars in research, infrastructure, and engineering to build this model. Without your work, this output would not exist. Training on publicly available data is legal under current fair use doctrine. You have terms of service that users agree to.

**Your argument**: We own the model, we set the terms, and the output is either ours or the user's — not the artists in the training data.

**Key points to develop**:
- What legal precedents support training on public data?
- How is this different from a human musician who listened to a lot of Motown and then wrote a soulful ballad?
- What would you offer artists, if anything?

### Group 3 — The User

You wrote the prompt. You auditioned three results and chose the best one. You made creative and aesthetic judgments. You are the one taking the output into the world and deciding how to use it.

**Your argument**: The user who curated and directed the output exercised creative agency and should own the result, or at minimum share ownership.

**Key points to develop**:
- How much creative contribution is needed to claim authorship?
- Is a prompt analogous to a brief given to a human collaborator? To a sample cleared for use?
- Does it matter that you did not write code, did not train the model, and could not have produced this without the company and the training artists?

### Group 4 — The Public / No One

AI-generated works were not created by a human author; therefore they should not be eligible for copyright protection. The output should enter the public domain immediately. Music is a cultural commons, and training data was taken from that commons.

**Your argument**: No one should own this output; it should be freely available to everyone.

**Key points to develop**:
- What is the purpose of copyright? Does that purpose apply here?
- U.S. Copyright Office has said AI-generated works are not copyrightable if no human author is present — what counts as sufficient human authorship?
- What would happen to the incentive to build these tools if outputs cannot be owned?

## Straw Poll & Debrief

After the debate, the instructor runs a show-of-hands straw poll. You vote your personal position — you may have changed from your assigned group, or you may not have.

**Poll question**: Who should have the primary ownership claim over this AI-generated song?

- The human artists in the training data
- The AI company
- The user who wrote the prompt
- No one — it should be public domain
- Some combination (describe)

**Debrief questions**:
1. What existing legal frameworks came up? (Copyright, fair use, right of publicity, work-for-hire, sui generis)
2. What new framework might be needed that does not exist yet?
3. Did your position change during the debate? What argument moved you?
4. What does your answer imply for: a musician who cannot compete with free AI generation? For a user who wants to commercialize an AI song?

---

## Instructor Notes

**Assigning groups**: assign randomly or by seating; do not let students choose their position. The constraint to argue an assigned view is part of the exercise.

**Moderating the open debate**: watch for cross-talk; call on groups by name. If one group dominates, redirect: "Group 3, what do you say to that?" Keep the tone substantive — discourage rhetorical moves that do not engage the actual argument.

**Legal references to have ready**:
- *Thaler v. Perlmutter* (2023) — U.S. District Court held that purely AI-generated images cannot be copyrighted
- U.S. Copyright Office guidance on AI-generated works (2023 circular)
- RIAA lawsuits against Suno and Udio (2024) — training data and direct copying claims
- EU AI Act provisions on training data transparency
- "Style is not copyrightable" — longstanding U.S. doctrine (*Williams v. Gaye* complicates this for "feel")
